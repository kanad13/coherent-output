Use a facilitator and short rounds so everyone gets an opening before the discussion becomes free-flowing. Keep your settled format: six people, online, free tools, 90 minutes on the first Sunday, with members voting on the book. The structure below is my suggested default.

Before each meeting, choose one facilitator and prepare three open questions: a passage that stood out, a choice or argument members disagreed with, and something the book changed for them. Use your free call tool and its chat; no extra software is needed.

Run the 90 minutes as follows:

- **0–5 minutes: Set the rules.** Everyone gets a turn; passing is fine. Keep contributions to two minutes. During open discussion, type “next” in chat to join the speaking queue. Everyone who wants a first turn gets one before anyone takes a second.
- **5–17 minutes: Opening round.** Give each person up to two minutes to share their overall reaction. Hold replies until everyone has had their turn.
- **17–71 minutes: Three 18-minute discussion rounds.** For each question, allow one minute to think, then one minute per person for an initial response, followed by 11 minutes of queued discussion. Change who starts each round. Return to the first-turn rule for each new question.
- **71–83 minutes: Closing round.** Give each person up to two minutes for a takeaway or a point they wish had received more attention.
- **83–90 minutes: Next meeting.** Confirm the next first-Sunday meeting and the book-voting arrangements.

The facilitator must enforce the rules, including with friends. Use brief, neutral interruptions:

- “I’m going to pause you there so we can hear the next person.”
- “Let’s give everyone who wants to speak a first turn before coming back to you.”
- “We have three people waiting; I’ll take them in order.”

Apply these rules to everyone rather than singling out the two frequent speakers. Do not require quieter members to contribute; offer the turn and accept a pass. Rotate the facilitator monthly if someone else is willing, so managing airtime does not become one person’s permanent job.

This trades some spontaneity for predictable access to the conversation. Try it for two meetings. At the end of each, ask everyone to answer in chat: “Did you have enough room to contribute: yes, partly, or no?” Keep or adjust the structure based on that feedback, especially whether anyone who wanted to speak was repeatedly left waiting.
